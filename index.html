03 / Problem Solving

The obvious fix is rarely the real one.

Four problems that looked like one thing and turned out to be another. The pattern underneath is the same: pin the problem to a number, find the mechanism, fix the mechanism.

Billing · Hughes

The bill nobody could read

Looked like: customers keep calling about their bills — staff up the call center.

The real problem: the bills were genuinely hard to decode. I audited ~10,000 billing-call transcripts and found repeating confusion patterns no FAQ would fix. So I built an Interactive Bill Explainer — AWS text-to-speech walking customers through their own charges — and iterated it against measured behavior.

Result — 15% of billing support calls deflected.

Voice · Hughes

The “flaky network” that wasn’t

Looked like: recurring VoIP dropped calls — a network capacity problem.

The real problem: a technical root-cause analysis across customer complaints, SIP signaling, terminal logs, and network evidence showed the culprit was bandwidth-request behavior during peak congestion. I partnered with Engineering to validate it, then drove firmware validation, a staged rollout, and production scaling.

Result — dropped calls down ~10%, CSAT up ~15%.

Fulfillment · Hughes

The 3-hour modem swap

Looked like: customers hated the warranty replacement experience — a training problem.

The real problem: activating a replacement modem took three hours of manual beam-lock steps. Nobody had questioned the steps themselves. I redesigned the flow around a pre-configured 4-digit beam-lock code delivered in the shipment email, plus a firmware update reusing an existing progress-bar app.

Result — activation went from 3 hours to 10 minutes.

Payments · Hughes

The fee everyone accepted

Looked like: credit-card processing fees are just a cost of doing business.

The real problem: nobody had made the cheaper rail easy or worth switching to. I launched a credit-card-to-ACH pilot, proved it, then ran the full adoption program — messaging, incentives, and measurement included.

Result — 9% of the customer base moved to ACH.

04 / AI, Responsibly

Most AI demos never survive production. Mine do.

I’m a practitioner, not a theorist. I personally design, build, and test AI workflows — Copilot Studio agents, Power Automate flows, Gemini-assisted analysis — and I do the part most teams skip: agentic evaluation. Initial prompts, structured testing, prompt iteration, guardrails derived from what testing actually surfaced, and human-in-the-loop where the stakes demand it.

To make that repeatable, I wrote two artifacts I’m proud of: a plain-English guide to the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and a 10-week AI governance training plan — built from deploying real systems inside a regulated-ish telecom environment, not from reading about them.

My rule is simple: if you can’t evaluate it, you can’t govern it — and if you can’t govern it, you shouldn’t ship it.
